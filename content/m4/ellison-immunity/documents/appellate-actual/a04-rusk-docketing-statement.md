# Officer Rusk's Docketing Statement and Collateral-Order Jurisdiction Statement

Filed January 28, 2026.

This appeal arises from an individual-capacity action under 42 U.S.C. § 1983. Mara Ellison alleges
that Officer Nolan Rusk used excessive force during a welfare-check seizure on February 3, 2025.
Rusk moved for summary judgment on the Fourth Amendment merits and qualified immunity. The
district court denied the motion on January 9, 2026, and Rusk filed his notice seven days later.

The order identifies a genuine dispute about whether Ellison struck Rusk's forearm and reached
toward his duty belt or instead only recoiled and pulled from painful wrist control. It separately
holds that, accepting Ellison's version, one five-second drive-stun cycle and a forcible takedown
violated clearly established law. Rusk appeals the latter legal conclusion and preserves his
contention that the objective recordings affect the set of facts properly assumed.

No final judgment has entered. The district court has jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1331. Rusk
invokes 28 U.S.C. § 1291 and the collateral-order doctrine only to the extent governing law permits
immediate review of a qualified-immunity denial.

# Basis and limits of appellate jurisdiction

A denial of qualified immunity conclusively rejects an immunity from suit, concerns an issue
separate from the claim's ultimate merits, and would be effectively unreviewable after trial when
the appeal presents a legal question. Rusk therefore asks whether the facts the district court
assumed describe a constitutional violation and whether pre-incident precedent placed that answer
beyond debate.

Rusk recognizes the jurisdictional limit stated in *Johnson v. Jones*, 515 U.S. 304 (1995). This
Court may not use an interlocutory appeal merely to decide whether the summary-judgment record is
sufficient for a jury to find particular historical facts. Ellison is expected to argue that any
reference to the video obstruction or audio crosses that line. Rusk will separate his legal
argument on assumed facts from his narrower submission that *Scott v. Harris*, 550 U.S. 372
(2007), permits a court to disregard a version blatantly contradicted by objective media.

The district court found that no such contradiction exists. Whether an appellate court may revisit
that characterization at this stage is itself contested. Rusk's identification of the issue does
not ask the Clerk to resolve it. Any portion beyond collateral-order authority may be dismissed
without defeating review of the independent legal immunity question.

# Questions anticipated for review

First, does the Court possess interlocutory jurisdiction to consider Rusk's submission that the
synchronized recordings constrain the factual assumptions, where the critical interval is
visually obstructed and the district court found the audio nonconclusive?

Second, accepting that Ellison was unarmed and distressed, pulled from wrist control, but did not
strike, grab, flee, reach toward police equipment, or pose an immediate danger, did one five-second
drive-stun cycle during a forcible descent violate the Fourth Amendment?

Third, if that force was unreasonable, did decisions existing by February 3, 2025 clearly
establish the unlawfulness of a single contact-mode cycle in this physical-control setting?

Rusk does not challenge the initial seizure's lawfulness. He does not seek review of municipal or
official-capacity claims omitted from the amended complaint. The district court's finding that a
jury could accept either historical account is relevant to the jurisdictional boundary but is not
a merits judgment against Rusk.

The desired disposition is reversal on qualified immunity. Alternatively, Rusk seeks resolution
of the legal issue within the Court's jurisdiction while leaving disputed facts for proceedings
below. Ellison may request dismissal of any evidence-sufficiency component and affirmance of the
assumed-facts legal ruling.
