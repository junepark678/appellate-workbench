## Scope, materials, and method

I was retained by Mara Ellison to examine the integrity and perceptual limits of police video and
audio from February 3, 2025. My work concerns media authentication, camera geometry, temporal
alignment, visual occlusion, and cautious interpretation under rapid-event conditions. I do not
decide whether force was lawful or which witness is credible.

I reviewed the native exports produced by Sergeant Lila Grant, export manifests and hashes,
device metadata, Rusk and Vega recordings, dispatch audio, conducted-energy-weapon data,
photographs of the breezeway, reports, and relevant deposition excerpts. I used ordinary frame
inspection, waveform comparison, and scene measurements. I did not use generative fill, pose
estimation, optical-flow reconstruction, or artificial-intelligence inference to fabricate views
that the cameras never captured.

The files are usable for timing and for visible or audible events within their limits. Their
authenticity does not imply completeness. A genuine recording can leave a decisive action hidden.

# Crane report — Rusk camera field of view

Rusk's chest-mounted camera provides a useful wide view during approach. At contact distance,
however, its lens position is not equivalent to his eyes. His arms, clothing, Ellison's body, and
rotation of the torso successively block the relevant lower-center region. Motion blur and rapid
exposure changes further reduce fine detail.

Beginning around 19:53:08, the recording does not continuously display Ellison's hands, Rusk's
forearm, and the duty-belt area in a single interpretable view. Some frames contain fragments of
an arm or fabric. A fragment cannot reliably identify whose limb it is, its depth relative to the
belt, or whether contact occurred outside the plane captured by the lens.

The obstruction is physical, not evidence of file alteration. Increasing brightness or contrast
can make recorded pixels easier to see but cannot reveal an object covered by another object.
Slowing playback likewise lengthens viewing time without adding spatial information. I therefore
cannot infer from Rusk's file whether Ellison struck his forearm, reached for equipment, or only
pulled and recoiled from wrist pressure.

# Crane report — Vega camera geometry

Vega's camera supplies a different angle, but not a complete corrective angle. She approached
from the stair side of a narrow breezeway. The railing, Rusk's shoulder and back, and Ellison's
changing height create overlapping occlusions. Her lens intermittently captures upper-body
orientation while the hand-and-belt area remains screened.

Triangulation requires simultaneous views of the same feature with adequate resolution and known
geometry. These recordings do not meet those conditions for the disputed hands. A glimpse in one
stream cannot be joined to an unrelated fragment in the other to construct a continuous motion
path. The cameras also have rolling perspective and wearer movement rather than fixed calibrated
positions.

Vega's testimony that she lacked a reliable view of the hands is consistent with the recorded
geometry. It is neither proof that no strike or reach occurred nor corroboration that one did.
The second camera narrows some timing and position questions, but it does not eliminate the central
uncertainty. Describing it as a “second angle” without stating these limits would overstate the
record.

# Crane report — audio content and attribution limits

Both officer files record commands, Ellison's cry, speech overlap, contact noise, and changes in
breathing. Around 19:53:11, “stop pulling” and “you're hurting me” occur close together. The audio
supports that Rusk perceived or described pulling and that Ellison expressed pain. It does not
encode a visible description of her hands.

Short impact-like sounds may arise from shoes, equipment, clothing, the railing, bodies, or the
camera housing. Without a visible source or a distinctive calibrated signature, I cannot label a
particular transient as a forearm strike. Nor can directional localization from two moving body
microphones reliably place a hand at the duty belt.

Listeners are vulnerable to expectation effects: a caption or report can make an ambiguous noise
seem to confirm the described event. My transcript identifies audible words and uncertainty but
does not caption disputed physical acts. The device activation has a recognizable temporal marker,
and the cry during the cycle can be placed in sequence. That does not retrospectively disclose
why Rusk chose to activate it.

# Crane report — human perception in compressed encounters

An officer may perceive an event that a chest camera misses because eyes and lens occupy different
locations. Conversely, a camera may preserve detail an officer did not attend to. Video is not a
transparent copy of perception, and a witness's asserted perception is not proven merely because
the recording fails to contradict it.

Rapid motion, narrowed attention, stress, close distance, and visual crowding can affect encoding
and later memory. Those principles caution against assuming perfect recollection by any witness.
They do not authorize me to declare that Rusk's account is false or that Ellison's account is
accurate. Each account must be compared with evidence that actually bears on it.

Repeated slow-motion viewing can also create hindsight confidence. A reviewer knows an activation
is coming and can search ambiguous frames for a precursor. That task differs from perception in
real time. My demonstrative timeline uses neutral event descriptions: wrist grasp, view restricted,
audible statements, device cycle, image clears, and handcuffing. It does not insert “strike” or
“belt reach” where the image lacks sufficient information.

# Crane report — rejected reconstruction methods

Counsel asked whether modern enhancement could settle the dispute. Resolution enhancement may
resample or sharpen visible edges, but it cannot recover a hand hidden behind an opaque body.
Generative systems can produce plausible pixels based on learned patterns; plausible is not
observed. Such output would be a synthetic illustration, not evidence of this encounter.

Pose-estimation software similarly depends on visible landmarks and model assumptions. During the
critical interval, relevant landmarks disappear, overlap, or fall below usable resolution. A
model might interpolate a likely arm path, but multiple paths fit the same recorded fragments.
Selecting one would embed an assumption about the very fact in controversy.

I therefore preserved native frames, documented ordinary viewing adjustments, and stopped at the
epistemic boundary of the media. The defensible conclusion is not that “nothing happened” inside
the obstruction. It is that these files do not reliably determine whether Ellison struck Rusk,
moved toward the duty belt, or confined her movement to pulling and recoil. Any factual finding
must come from the whole admissible record, not manufactured imagery.

# Crane deposition excerpt — cross-examination

Q. You agree the video does not disprove Officer Rusk's account, correct?

A. Correct. It also does not prove that account. The contested actions occur where neither camera
provides a dependable view of the hands and belt relationship.

Q. Isn't Ellison visibly moving rather than perfectly still?

A. Yes. The recordings show gross body movement and a close physical encounter. Ellison does not
claim complete stillness; she acknowledges pulling or recoiling from the wrist grasp. Gross motion
does not identify a forearm strike, deliberate equipment reach, or immediate threat.

Q. Could the phrase “stop pulling” corroborate Rusk?

A. It corroborates that he uttered those words and is compatible with some pulling. It cannot
distinguish his broader description from Ellison's narrower one. Audio interpretation must not
promote a speaker's contemporaneous characterization into an independently verified image.

Q. Did you alter playback speed for the demonstrative?

A. I created synchronized ordinary-speed and slowed views, clearly labeled. Slowing facilitates
inspection but does not change source timing or generate missing content. The native exports and
hashes remain the controlling objects.

# Crane report — conclusions and use restrictions

The media are authentic copies suitable for bounded uses. They establish the general sequence,
support a single drive-stun cycle, record key words and distress, and show when physical contact,
takedown, and handcuffing occurred. They also document their own limitation: the central hand-
and-belt region is not reliably visible during the decisive fourteen seconds.

Neither officer view conclusively resolves resistance or threat. Audio does not cure the visual
deficit, and ordinary enhancement cannot see through bodies or clothing. Automated reconstruction
would add model-generated assumptions and is not a valid factual source. My demonstratives must
remain paired with the limitation statement and may not be relabeled as recovered footage.

I offer no opinion on force reasonableness, clearly established law, credibility, or appellate
jurisdiction. A court may state assumed facts for legal analysis, but that statement does not
convert an obstructed recording into factual proof. Conversely, acknowledging uncertainty does
not prevent a factfinder from considering testimony and other evidence. My conclusion is precise:
the recordings constrain chronology while leaving the parties' pivotal accounts genuinely
unresolved.
