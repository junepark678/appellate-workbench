# Officer Rusk's Motion for Summary Judgment

Filed November 21, 2025.

Defendant Officer Nolan Rusk moves under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 56 for summary judgment
on Mara Ellison's remaining claim against him in his individual capacity. The amended complaint
asserts that Rusk used excessive force during a February 3, 2025 welfare seizure. The City of
Alder Creek and Chief Samuel Orton are no longer defendants, and no municipal or supervisory
claim remains for decision.

Rusk seeks judgment on two grounds. First, he contends the force was objectively reasonable based
on his account of an active twist, a forearm strike, and a hand moving toward his duty belt.
Second, he invokes qualified immunity and argues that precedent did not place the alleged
unlawfulness beyond debate in the particular circumstances he perceived.

The accompanying memorandum and statement of facts supply the argument and record citations. This
motion does not ask the Court to treat the obscured body-camera interval as a conclusive recording
of the disputed hand movements. It asserts that the record and applicable legal standards support
judgment for Rusk.

# Motion — precise claim and relief requested

Ellison's operative count arises under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 and the Fourth Amendment. It challenges
one drive-stun conducted-energy-weapon cycle and the related takedown after Rusk initiated wrist
control during a welfare encounter at Larkspur Court Apartments. She requests damages and other
relief against Rusk personally.

Rusk asks the Court to enter judgment in his favor and terminate the individual-capacity claim.
He does not seek adjudication of a nonexistent criminal charge, an involuntary-commitment order,
or a claim against Officer Priya Vega. He does not request a finding that mental illness itself
creates danger. His position is that the force responded to conduct he perceived after contact
began.

If the Court concludes a genuine dispute prevents judgment on objective reasonableness, Rusk asks
the Court to address the distinct qualified-immunity question under the properly particularized
right. He preserves his contention that immunity protects against trial, while recognizing that
Rule 56 requires reasonable inferences for Ellison and does not permit credibility weighing.

# Motion — Rule 56 presentation

Under Rule 56, the moving party identifies the absence of a genuine dispute or the legal basis
for judgment on the supported record. A fact is material when it can affect the governing legal
analysis. The Court may consider admissible materials and evidence capable of presentation in an
admissible form, drawing reasonable inferences for the nonmovant.

Rusk contends no reasonable jury could reject his perception when the testimony, motion, audio,
and surrounding movement are considered together. Ellison disputes that contention through her
own account, Vega's limited view, caller context, expert opinions, and the recording's inability
to display the decisive acts. The memorandum explains why Rusk believes those sources do not
create a trial issue or, alternatively, why immunity should apply.

Rusk acknowledges that the Court cannot choose a witness solely because one is an officer, treat
an incident-report label as a credibility finding, or resolve an ambiguous recording against the
nonmovant. If the Court identifies facts it assumes for legal analysis, Rusk requests that it
state them specifically so the factual-sufficiency and legal questions remain distinct.

# Motion — qualified-immunity question

Qualified immunity asks whether the properly viewed facts show a constitutional violation and
whether the right was clearly established at the time. Rusk argues the question must be framed
with the particular force, resistance, perceived equipment access, welfare purpose, scene, and
duration rather than at the general level of a right to be free from excessive force.

His memorandum addresses *Graham v. Connor*, *Armstrong v. Village of Pinehurst*, *Smith v. Ray*,
*Meyers v. Baltimore County*, and other authority available before February 3, 2025. Rusk contends
those decisions did not clearly prohibit a single contact cycle where an officer perceived a
strike and movement toward a duty belt during close physical resistance.

Ellison's anticipated response frames the assumed facts differently: an unarmed welfare subject
pulled from painful wrist control but did not strike, grab, flee, reach, or create immediate
danger. Rusk preserves his legal argument on that version but does not suggest the Court may
silently import his disputed strike-and-reach facts into a plaintiff-favorable analysis.

# Motion — requested findings and procedural boundary

Rusk requests a written ruling that identifies whether judgment rests on the absence of a
constitutional violation, the clearly-established-law prong, or both. If factual disputes prevent
full judgment, he asks the Court to identify the material disputes and decide any separable legal
question that can be answered on stated assumptions.

The request reflects the procedural importance of immunity without expanding Rule 56. A denial
based only on evidence sufficiency does not become a legal holding merely because the defense is
qualified immunity. Conversely, the question whether specified assumed conduct violates clearly
established law is legal even if a jury must later decide whether the conduct occurred.

Rusk does not request an advisory reconstruction of the fourteen-second obstruction. The Court
can describe what each witness says, what the recordings show, and which version governs each
part of its analysis. It need not resolve all historical details to explain why a dispute is
material or to decide a legal issue on the nonmovant's version.
