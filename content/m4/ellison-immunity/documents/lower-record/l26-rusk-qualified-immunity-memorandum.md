## Introduction

Officer Nolan Rusk responded to a welfare call and encountered Mara Ellison in an apartment
breezeway. When she would not cooperate with assessment, Rusk took wrist control. He testifies
that she actively twisted, struck his forearm, and moved a hand toward his duty belt. He used one

The Fourth Amendment judges that decision from the perspective of an officer in a tense, rapidly
evolving encounter. It does not require certainty before an officer protects access to weapons.
On Rusk's account, the brief force was reasonable. Qualified immunity independently applies
because no controlling precedent clearly prohibited this limited response to a perceived strike
and equipment reach during close physical resistance.

Ellison denies the strike and reach. Rusk maintains the record does not create a genuine dispute,
but the alternative analysis below states the legal question on her version rather than treating
the obstructed video as conclusive.

# Memorandum — Rule 56 standard

Summary judgment is proper when no genuine dispute of material fact exists and the movant is
entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(a). The nonmovant receives reasonable
inferences, and the Court does not weigh credibility. *Tolan v. Cotton*, 572 U.S. 650, 656–57
(2014). A supported factual dispute must be genuine, not speculative.

Video can prevent a court from adopting a version that the recording blatantly contradicts.
*Scott v. Harris*, 550 U.S. 372, 380 (2007). But this is not a case in which a continuous image
plainly depicts the disputed conduct. Authenticity establishes that the camera files are genuine;
it does not turn their physical obstruction into a visible event.

Rusk contends his sworn account, contemporaneous command, reported forearm contact, close movement,
and prompt limited response outweigh Ellison's denial. The Court nonetheless must treat a
competent contradictory account as evidence if a reasonable jury could credit it. Should the
Court find a trial dispute, it may still determine qualified immunity on an expressly stated
plaintiff-favorable version without deciding who is truthful.

# Memorandum — objective reasonableness framework

Excessive-force claims during a seizure are governed by objective reasonableness. *Graham v.
Connor*, 490 U.S. 386, 395–97 (1989). The analysis considers the severity of suspected conduct,
whether the person poses an immediate threat, and whether she actively resists or attempts flight,
along with the total circumstances. It avoids hindsight and recognizes that officers often make
split-second choices.

The call's welfare purpose, absence of a reported weapon, and Ellison's distress favor measured
tactics. They do not freeze the assessment at dispatch. A threat can emerge when contact begins.
If Ellison struck Rusk and reached toward equipment, the immediate-threat and active-resistance
considerations changed within seconds.

The challenged force was bounded. Rusk used contact mode during the takedown, not projectile
probes at a distance; the device recorded one cycle; he did not repeat it after the view cleared;
and officers transitioned to cuffs and medical care. The mode remains painful force, but duration,
purpose, and cessation bear on proportionality. The question is whether the perceived danger
justified that brief escalation at the moment it occurred.

# Memorandum — safety interest under Rusk's account

Police duty belts hold tools and weapons. During close contact, a hand moving toward that area
can create an immediate safety risk even if the person was unarmed before the movement. An officer
need not identify the exact item sought or permit access before acting, provided the perception
and response are objectively reasonable.

Rusk testifies that Ellison twisted against control, struck his forearm, and moved toward his
belt. The breezeway limited lateral space, and Vega had not yet secured the other hand. Rusk used
the cycle while directing Ellison down, stopped after one activation, and completed restraint.
That account links the force to interruption of equipment access rather than punishment for
mental illness or verbal disagreement.

Vega did not see the hands clearly. Her limited view neither corroborates nor refutes Rusk. The
lack of a visible forearm injury also does not exclude contact. A reasonable officer may respond
to a strike and reach without waiting to learn whether the strike causes lasting injury. On
Rusk's version, the governmental safety need outweighs the brief, localized application.

# Memorandum — resistance, distress, and alternatives

Rusk does not contend that distress alone authorizes force. Nor does he argue every failure to

Ellison emphasizes de-escalation options. Officers could continue speaking, change position, ask

Dr. Dana North agrees that Rusk's asserted strike-and-reach premise materially changes the threat

If the Court assumes Ellison was unarmed, pulled from a painful wrist grasp, did not strike,

# Memorandum — qualified-immunity doctrine

Qualified immunity protects an official unless the facts show violation of a constitutional
right that was clearly established when the conduct occurred. *Pearson v. Callahan*, 555 U.S.
223, 231–36 (2009). Courts may address either prong first. The right must be defined in a
particularized manner rather than as a general freedom from unreasonable force. *Ashcroft v.
al-Kidd*, 563 U.S. 731, 741–42 (2011).

Clearly established law need not contain a case identical in every respect, but existing
precedent must give fair notice that the specific conduct is unlawful. General propositions may
control an obvious case, while factual distinctions matter when reasonableness depends on danger,
resistance, force type, and timing.

Rusk frames the defense-favorable question as whether, by February 3, 2025, precedent clearly
forbade one drive-stun contact cycle during a takedown when an officer reasonably perceived an
active strike and a reach toward duty-belt equipment. He submits that no controlling case does.
If the Court uses Ellison's facts, it should separately ask whether the more limited pulling and
absence of danger place the event inside a clearly established prohibition.

# Memorandum — cited Fourth Circuit decisions

*Armstrong v. Village of Pinehurst*, 810 F.3d 892 (4th Cir. 2016), explains that resistance is
not binary and that serious force requires attention to immediate danger. It addressed repeated
tasing of a stationary person clinging to a post during an involuntary-commitment seizure and
held the law at the 2011 event was not then clearly established. Its reasoning informed officers
before 2025, but Rusk argues a perceived strike and belt reach supply the danger missing there.

*Meyers v. Baltimore County*, 713 F.3d 723 (4th Cir. 2013), distinguishes taser uses as threat
conditions change. *Smith v. Ray*, 781 F.3d 95 (4th Cir. 2015), addresses force after modest
movement by a person who, on the credited facts, gave no reason to appear dangerous. *Yates v.
Terry*, 817 F.3d 877 (4th Cir. 2016), likewise involves force against a nonthreatening subject.

These cases support Ellison if her assumptions control. They do not clearly prohibit the response
on Rusk's asserted equipment-access facts. The Court should avoid using a precedent premised on
no immediate danger while silently deciding the disputed danger question against him.

# Memorandum — injuries and force magnitude

The medical record identifies a wrist abrasion, two superficial contact burns, shoulder strain,
and no fracture. Rusk does not deny observable injury. The absence of fracture is relevant to
force magnitude but not dispositive; Fourth Amendment protection is not limited to catastrophic
harm. Similarly, the existence of injury does not prove the force lacked justification.

Paramedic Mateo Cole did not witness the encounter and cannot reconstruct hand positions from the
injury pattern. He accurately separates patient history from examination. The marks corroborate
device contact, which Rusk admits, while leaving the reason for activation unresolved.

The response was shorter and narrower than repeated cycles or force continuing after restraint.
Once officers gained control, Rusk did not activate the device again. Medical evaluation followed.
Those facts matter to the totality and to fair notice. Ellison may argue even one cycle is excessive
without immediate danger; Rusk's defense is that immediate danger existed as he perceived it and
that, on any version, the confined encounter and limited duration prevent the case from matching
precedent with sufficient clarity to remove immunity.

# Memorandum — conclusion

The Court should grant summary judgment. On Rusk's supported account, an active strike and
movement toward duty-belt equipment created an immediate safety need. One drive-stun cycle during

responding.

Qualified immunity provides an independent basis. No precedent available on February 3, 2025
clearly prohibited that limited response to the specific danger Rusk describes. The cases Ellison
invokes depend on subjects who, under the facts credited there, did not pose a comparable immediate
threat.

If the Court finds the strike and reach genuinely disputed, it should identify Ellison's assumed
facts precisely. Rusk maintains immunity even then based on the encounter's confined setting,
ongoing wrist-control resistance, one-cycle duration, and lack of a materially identical case. At
minimum, the ruling should keep that legal contention distinct from any conclusion that a jury
must decide what occurred behind the camera obstruction.
