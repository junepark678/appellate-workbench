# Merits hearing transcript — volume 2
## September 18, 2024 reconvening

The immigration court reconvenes at 9:00 a.m. with the same parties and interpreter.
The judge confirms that volume one ended after A.R.M.'s testimony and that the exhibit receipt
records P-7 as admitted. Neither party identifies a transcription error affecting that ruling.

Counsel agree that S.M. and Nadia Pel will testify through the initials-and-role protocol. Ilyan
Varo will testify as a country-conditions expert subject to qualification. DHS will call
one institutional-policy witness. The judge repeats that evidence is not accepted for the truth of
an expert's assumed facts unless supported on an independent foundation.

# S.M. sworn and relationship foundation

S.M. is sworn and identifies as A.R.M.'s older sibling. They lived in the same family residence in
Orin Vale during May through August 2023. S.M. worked evening shifts and kept a household calendar
for appointments and received messages. The calendar and communication log were prepared
contemporaneously, though the declaration explaining them was signed later.

S.M. was not present during the asserted detention and cannot identify who held A.R.M. The witness
can describe departure, return, observable condition, the clinic visit, the complaint preparation,
and the July encounter. Counsel expressly limits questions to those subjects.

<!-- PAGE BREAK -->
# Observations after release

S.M. testifies that A.R.M. failed to return home on May 19 and 20. On May 21, A.R.M. arrived walking
slowly, with dark wrist marks, a swollen right ankle, limited movement in the left shoulder, and
discomfort while lowering into a chair. S.M. photographed the visible condition with a household
device and logged the file controls. The source descriptions and margins are preserved in the
family packet.

A.R.M. said municipal officers had demanded procurement copies. S.M. offers that statement only as
part of the contemporaneous report and A.R.M.'s state, not as firsthand identification. The witness
accompanied A.R.M. to the North Bell clinic, which registered the visit on May 21 at 14:20.

<!-- PAGE BREAK -->
# Complaint preparation and July encounter

S.M. helped organize the complaint submitted June 7. The packet named Unit Four and described the
ledger demand. S.M. saw the neutral receipt and later the routing extract. No person from an
external review desk contacted the household. The extract ended June 30 with “regional response
pending” at `RC-4` and no interview.

On July 11, S.M. was beside A.R.M. when two people approached near the residence. One mentioned
the copied book and Harbor League; the other warned that the household could be found after a move.
S.M. could not identify either person by name or official role. The words and location, not an
institutional conclusion, are the firsthand evidence.

<!-- PAGE BREAK -->
# Cross-examination of S.M.

DHS establishes that S.M. and A.R.M. are close, discussed the claim often, and reviewed the

S.M. agrees that the July speakers wore no visible government insignia and that Harbor League is an

On redirect, S.M. explains that the log was started before any United States proceeding and

The judge asks whether anyone suggested adding an officer's name. S.M. answers no and reiterates

# Nadia Pel qualifications and municipal role

Nadia Pel is sworn. She served as an Orin Vale municipal compliance officer from 2018 through
December 2023. Her duties included reviewing procurement audit trails, authenticating routing
tables, and referring complaints involving municipal personnel. She had no authority to decide
criminal liability or order detention.

Pel recognizes the procurement-register format, approval-path codes, and Civic Integrity routing
table in the family packet. Her authentication is based on work responsibilities and retained
training copies, not on information supplied after leaving Kalyra. DHS reserves objections to the
weight of copied municipal material.

<!-- PAGE BREAK -->
# Ledger versions and contact overlap

Pel compares the two register versions. They list six Harbor League invoices, NQL-3101 through
NQL-3106, each below the 25,000-unit secondary-review threshold and totaling 137,400 units. The
first version groups all six under maintenance cycle `NQ-APR-A`; the corrected version removes that
grouping and labels each invoice an urgent standalone repair. Pel cannot say whether the change was
an innocent correction or an effort to avoid review.

One vendor contact number matches a dispatch number Pel saw on a municipal framework list. A former
Unit Four logistics supervisor was associated with that dispatch office. Pel explains the source
of the match and concedes that association does not establish a command relationship or unlawful
conduct.

<!-- PAGE BREAK -->
# Complaint-routing protocol

Pel explains the written Civic Integrity protocol. A complaint naming the receiving security unit
should go to an external review desk. In practice, Orin Vale staff were instructed to mark
procurement-security complaints with an operational-security code. The routing table sent that
code to the Merrow regional command, where Unit Four had a liaison.

Pel objected internally that the practice defeated independent review. A supervisor responded that
the regional command could decide whether outside referral was necessary. Pel retained a blank
routing table and training memorandum; neither document names A.R.M.

<!-- PAGE BREAK -->
# Routing of A.R.M.'s complaint

Pel recognizes complaint `OCI-23-441`, accepted June 7 under initial route `IC-2`. The June 9
entry links it to `CIV-23-184` through `ARM-RX-14`; the June 10 entry changes destination to `RC-4`,
Unit Four regional command; and the June 30 status is “regional response pending,” with no
interview. Pel did not process this specific complaint because she had already been moved from
intake duties, but she can interpret the codes through her prior work.

The judge admits the routing explanation for code meaning while reserving the inference about who
read the complaint. Pel cannot testify that a particular Unit Four officer received it. She can
testify that the institutional destination was the command containing that liaison.

<!-- PAGE BREAK -->
# Cross-examination of Pel on safeguards

DHS elicits that Kalyrian law prohibits retaliation and that an inspector hotline exists. Pel used
the hotline once on an unrelated procurement matter and received an acknowledgment. She cannot
say that every regional referral is ignored or that the external desk lacks authority.

Pel also acknowledges leaving municipal employment after a management dispute and receiving a
fixed fee for testimony preparation. The retained training copies omit full personnel names.
DHS suggests that the operational-security code may protect sensitive infrastructure. Pel agrees
that it has a legitimate purpose but maintains that it should not return a complaint to a named
unit without outside screening.

<!-- PAGE BREAK -->
# Redirect of Pel

Counsel asks whether a formal safeguard answers what occurred in this file. Pel says the hotline
and external desk show available structures, while the visible codes show that this complaint used
a different path. The distinction is between legal authority and actual routing, not a claim that
all remedies are useless.

Pel confirms that her testimony does not prove A.R.M.'s detention. It authenticates register and
routing formats, explains a contact overlap, and identifies the destination and pending-status
meaning of the complaint extract. The judge excuses Pel after DHS declines further questions.

# Qualification of Ilyan Varo

Varo is sworn and summarizes experience studying Kalyrian procurement, security, and
complaint systems. Counsel offers the expert in institutional structure and documented practice,
not medicine, credibility, or legal standards. DHS questions source selection, coded interviews,
and prior criticism of translated press reliance.

The judge finds Varo qualified within the stated scope. The qualification does not guarantee the
accuracy of each source. Varo must identify assumptions, contrary material, and the difference
between capability and a predicted event. The twenty-page report and source register remain in
evidence under the prior exhibit receipt, with Varo's testimony subject to those limits.

<!-- PAGE BREAK -->
# Varo's method explained

Varo describes the hierarchy of directives, authenticated registers, coded interviews, and
corroborated summaries. Formal rules are given full weight as evidence of lawful structure. Practice
claims require a document pattern or more than one independent account. Missing data are not
silently treated as adverse.

The expert reviewed A.R.M.'s materials only to map asserted pathways. Varo did not assume a
detention occurred, did not interview A.R.M., and did not assign a probability. The source cutoff
precedes the agency decision, and no material arising after that cutoff was considered.

<!-- PAGE BREAK -->
# Unit Four capability and practice

Varo explains Unit Four's formal infrastructure-fraud mandate and the limits in Directive 18.
Terminal audits show practical access to employment, travel, and household-link fields through a
regional liaison. Some queries lack an identifiable registered case. Former employees described
questioning at an unlisted compound with features similar to A.R.M.'s account.

The sources establish capability and a practice pattern, not that A.R.M. has a current flag or
that every described officer acted unlawfully. Varo considers lawful oversight rules contrary
evidence. The judge asks that those limits appear in any finding based on the report.

<!-- PAGE BREAK -->
# Harbor League and registry links

Varo identifies overlap between Harbor League vendor contacts, a dispatch office, and a former
Unit Four logistics supervisor. Reports of messengers knowing complaint details are consistent but
vary on uniforms. Registry rules permit return notifications and household queries through a
regional liaison, though a proper query requires a case and approval.

The asserted chain therefore has connected nodes: contractor information, registry identification,
and official detention capacity. Varo cautions that overlap is not proof of a single conspiracy.
The same encounter should not be multiplied simply because several nodes contribute.

<!-- PAGE BREAK -->
# Complaint system and protective response

The forty-entry municipal sample shows most complaints naming security personnel routed to a
regional command rather than an external desk. None of those regional files records a complainant
interview; several close on the named unit's response. The sample is not nationally representative
and was selected because data were available.

Varo concludes only that formal independent review did not consistently operate in the sampled
files. A.R.M.'s routing code fits that pattern. Whether senior officials had actual knowledge of
the specific complaint is not established. Institutional receipt and response remain distinct from
individual mental state.

<!-- PAGE BREAK -->
# Formal prohibitions and enforcement gap

DHS asks Varo to describe safeguards. The expert confirms national prohibitions, an inspector
hotline, discipline tables, and lawful relocation. Those measures may reduce risk. The discipline
tables, however, combine minor and serious violations and disclose few outside referrals from the
hotline. A revised circular had been announced but implementation records were unavailable.

Varo does not equate a low referral count with approval of abuse. The narrower observation is that
formal rules cannot reveal whether a complaint routed to the implicated command received an
independent response. Both the rules and the operational evidence belong in the assessment.

<!-- PAGE BREAK -->
# Cross-examination on sample limits

DHS establishes that interview identities are coded, the complaint sample is small, and some
press summaries rely on translation. Varo agrees. No source states that A.R.M. personally appears
on a watch list. No authenticated order directs harm to procurement clerks. Several former clerks
experienced only requests to return documents.

Varo also agrees that lawful departure is contrary evidence and that a year without direct contact
may matter. The report does not convert uncertainty into certainty. It identifies a possible chain
whose weight depends on whether the underlying personal events and institutional links are credited.

<!-- PAGE BREAK -->
# Cross-examination on relocation

DHS asks whether A.R.M. could live outside Merrow Province. Varo confirms that no internal passport
blocks movement and that short-term cash lodging exists. A person avoiding registered work and
services may reduce visibility. Varo has no evidence of continuous national surveillance by Unit
Four.

The expert adds that entry data, formal employment, and household links use national or provincial
systems accessible through the liaison channel. Relocation may weaken local contact but does not
necessarily sever the registry pathway. The judge reserves whether those practical constraints
make relocation relevant under the governing CAT inquiry.

<!-- PAGE BREAK -->
# Redirect on aggregation and limits

Counsel asks Varo to distinguish adding probabilities from evaluating a connected chain. Varo says
independent pathways may contribute separately, while overlapping steps should be assessed once.
Registry notice, contractor leads, and Unit Four action are parts of one possible sequence. A
separate household threat may be independent evidence but may also share actors.

Varo repeats the limits: no personal flag, no universal contractor command, incomplete discipline
data, and no legal conclusion. The report's contribution is an institutional map against which the
judge can evaluate all admitted evidence rather than treating each feared actor in isolation.

# Oral summaries and submission

A.R.M. argues that prior detention, complaint routing, Harbor League access, and registry capacity
must be considered together and that formal prohibitions do not resolve file-specific response.
DHS argues that credibility gaps, lawful departure, time, relocation, and legal safeguards prevent

The judge takes the matter under advisement at 4:42 p.m. and states that a decision will address
