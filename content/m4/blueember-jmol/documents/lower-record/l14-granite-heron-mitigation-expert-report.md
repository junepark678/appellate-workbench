Calder identified no known incompatibility that would prevent BE-214 production. Cobalt Finch had an appropriate scale range, single-use processing capability, frozen receiving and storage, trained staff, analytical options, and a quality system designed for development campaigns.

BE-214 had not previously run there. That fact created transfer work, not a conclusion of infeasibility. Blue Ember’s own diligence did not uncover a fatal deficiency during the offer window. Its questions concerned how and when open items would be resolved. Cobalt Finch proposed a focused on-site review, staged documents, an engineering confirmation, and a joint November 22 readiness gate.

An established site such as Aurora Quay offered greater execution confidence. Economic reasonableness, however, does not require selecting only the lowest-risk path when a modest-cost alternative offers a large schedule benefit. The comparison must weigh likelihood, downside, cost, and loss threatened. The Cobalt Finch expected benefit substantially exceeded the incremental commitment.

## Decision analysis at October 4

A proper mitigation assessment uses information known when Blue Ember had to choose. By October 4, it knew the magnitude of threatened delay, had a held December slot, possessed a preliminary fit assessment, understood major transfer tasks, and had a 300,000-dollar incremental estimate.

Blue Ember also knew that Cobalt Finch was not yet program-qualified, that detailed review remained, and that some commitment could be lost if readiness failed. Those concerns were genuine. Blue Ember did not complete an on-site review or obtain a quantified risk assessment before declining. It favored the established site largely because of execution confidence. In my opinion, a reasonable organization facing this benefit-cost ratio would reserve the slot and promptly complete the planned diligence rather than surrender the capacity.

The decision did not require unsafe release or skipping change control. The proposal expressly retained readiness conditions. If a critical gap emerged, Blue Ember could stop, incur defined sunk cost, and return to its later path. That downside was far smaller than the time-related loss at risk.

For the successful alternative, 300,000 dollars is the appropriate incremental cost deducted from loss avoided. It should not be added to actual damages because Blue Ember did not incur it. It should not be subtracted twice as both an expense and a risk adjustment. My calculation identifies it in one line.

## Derivation of 1.8 million dollars avoided

Brooks attributes 2 million dollars to the actual timing shift. I mapped the December manufacturing path through testing and milestone effects rather than assuming all delay disappeared on December 2. The earlier course would have eliminated approximately ninety percent of the claimed time-related loss.

The remaining 200,000 dollars reflects disruption that would persist even with prompt mitigation: the September investigation, transfer mobilization, certain unavoidable schedule friction, and residual timing before replacement disposition. Thus gross delay avoided is 1.8 million dollars, not the full 2 million.

The calculation uses Blue Ember’s own risk-adjusted contribution structure. I did not pair an optimistic alternative with an unadjusted commercial forecast. Milestones are advanced only where the proposed schedule affects them, and later unrelated program uncertainty remains in the model.

A catastrophic transfer-failure scenario would avoid less, but the defined capability and task evidence make it an insufficient basis to treat the opportunity as worthless. Sensitivity testing shows that even substantial reduction in expected schedule benefit leaves the decision economically favorable relative to the 300,000-dollar incremental cost.

## Net mitigation deduction

The net calculation is 1.8 million dollars of avoidable delay minus 300,000 dollars required to obtain the alternative. The difference is 1.5 million dollars. Applied to the conditional 4.2 million dollar gross figure, the resulting loss is 2.7 million dollars.

The deduction concerns only avoidable delay. The original 1.35 million dollar lot cost and the 850,000 dollar replacement subtotal remain in the conditional result. That structure prevents the alternative expense from erasing an unrelated direct category.

My opinion treats the transfer cost as necessary even if some individual task later proved unnecessary. Using the full estimate is conservative for Blue Ember. It also leaves 200,000 dollars of the claimed delay in place rather than assuming perfect schedule recovery.

Granite Heron disputes damages beyond this mitigation issue. If the factfinder independently lowers gross delay below 1.8 million, the net deduction cannot exceed the supported avoidable portion. If it rejects one direct component, that change should occur in a different calculation line. The table is designed to reflect those findings without an overlapping reduction.

## Response to Brooks on uncertainty

Brooks emphasizes that no contemporaneous numerical success probability existed. Operations decisions commonly proceed without a single formal probability. Evidence of capability, task completion, known gaps, schedule margin, and downside allows a professional assessment even when the sponsor did not write a percentage in real time.

His approach effectively assigns too much weight to the possibility of failure and too little to the value of securing scarce capacity. Blue Ember could have managed uncertainty through staged diligence and the November readiness gate. Choosing not to pursue the offer transformed manageable risk into the certainty of waiting for March.

Brooks correctly states that probability should not be counted twice. My 1.8 million avoidance reflects residual schedule effect and uses the same risk-adjusted economic base as his model. I then subtract the entire 300,000-dollar incremental estimate. I do not add a second generic risk penalty.

He also correctly separates direct from delay categories. Our disagreement is therefore bounded: whether the Cobalt Finch evidence supports the earlier path strongly enough to require a substantial net reduction. In my opinion, the combined facts support 1.5 million dollars.

## Sensitivities and decision threshold

If gross avoidance were 1.8 million dollars and incremental cost 300,000 dollars, net benefit is 1.5 million. If avoidance were reduced to 1.2 million, the net benefit would still be 900,000. At 900,000 of avoidance, net benefit would be 600,000.

The financial break-even point is 300,000 dollars of gross avoidance, before considering nonfinancial program value. Granite Heron’s supported schedule evidence substantially exceeds that threshold. Even a cautious sponsor could use staged commitment to limit exposure while preserving the higher-value opportunity.

A 300,000-dollar verdict deduction would imply either only 600,000 dollars of gross avoidance after cost or another combination with the same net result. That is arithmetically possible, but it assigns markedly less schedule benefit than the Cobalt Finch calendar and Blue Ember’s own delay model support.

I do not instruct the factfinder which scenario the law permits. I show why the full net-benefit opinion is robust to ordinary uncertainty. A scenario based on total failure should be weighted against the known capability and proposed safeguards, not assumed merely because no guarantee existed.

## Separate issues and nonduplication

The shipment’s temperature chronology addresses performance and causation. Blue Ember’s quality disposition addresses whether the lot served its validation purpose. The cost schedules measure gross loss. Cobalt Finch evidence concerns what Blue Ember could reasonably do after rejection. These questions interact sequentially but are not the same issue.

An economic deduction does not revise the logger curve, excuse the power transition, or require the original lot to be released. Conversely, proof of a carrier breach does not answer whether a later option would reduce damages. The model keeps those determinations distinct.

The direct subtotal and the timing subtotal also remain separate. A reduction in delay does not revive the rejected asset. A credit for reused replacement work should affect direct cost and should not be counted again as mitigation. Insurance or vendor reimbursement should offset only the corresponding compensated item.

This modular structure allows a verdict form to identify gross damages and mitigation expressly. It also permits later review of one component without guessing whether a jury compromised liability, causation, direct cost, delay, or post-event conduct.
