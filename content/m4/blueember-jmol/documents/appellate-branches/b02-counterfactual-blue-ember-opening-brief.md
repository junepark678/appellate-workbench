Blue Ember Biologics, LLC v. Granite Heron Logistics, Inc.

Filed March 9, 2026

Blue Ember accepts the training order’s assumed preservation premise for this branch alone. The question is therefore merits, not continuity: could every reasonable jury presented with the Cobalt Finch record be compelled to impose Granite Heron’s entire $1,500,000 net mitigation deduction? If that relief were granted, Rule 50(c) would require attention to the district court’s conditional new-trial ruling. ## Questions presented

First, after assuming that Granite Heron expressly raised mitigation in both Rule 50(a) motions, did the district court still err by holding that no reasonable jury could select a net deduction below $1,500,000? The issue requires de novo review of the evidence in Blue Ember’s favor without weighing witnesses or choosing among competing economic inferences.

Second, if the mitigation JMOL is reversed, what consequence follows from the conditional Rule 59 ruling? Rule 50(c)(2) recognizes that a properly entered conditional new-trial determination may become operative when renewed judgment falls, subject to appellate review and direction.

Blue Ember preserves its objections while recognizing that these questions need not be decided if the Court affirms JMOL.

Fourth, what judgment should issue? Blue Ember requests relief tied to the component actually reversed. It does not ask the panel to alter contract liability or shipment causation, and it does not claim that the counterfactual premise changes the original district sources or licenses use of this branch as historical proof.

The mitigation issue therefore presented a mixed record: real opportunity, material schedule value, unfinished program work, and a meaningful chance of delay or failure.

## Blue Ember evaluated rather than ignored the option

Blue Ember’s technical, quality, operations, and finance personnel reviewed the proposal during the short hold. They requested an equipment matrix, analytical allocation, audit information, training plans, material lead times, cancellation terms, and the standard for the final readiness gate. Cobalt Finch answered what it could and explained which questions required controlled documents after acceptance.

The sponsor compared that accelerated route with Aurora Quay, an established facility reserved for March 3. An earlier start offered substantial upside, but a failed transfer could consume scarce personnel and staged expense while leaving the March campaign necessary. Blue Ember chose the later dependable course and released the Durham hold before expiration.

The decision file did not call Cobalt Finch incapable or legally prohibited. It credited general capability and potential acceleration while recording unresolved fit and execution confidence. That balanced assessment matters because mitigation asks whether the response was reasonable under circumstances known then, not whether later expert modeling can construct a successful alternative with hindsight.

Reasonable jurors could conclude that Blue Ember should have pursued some portion of the opportunity and still reject Park’s full outcome. That is exactly what the $300,000 net deduction reflects. The verdict need not correspond to either expert’s endpoint when the record permits intermediate judgments about probability, timing, and avoidable loss.

## The verdict separated every monetary component

The special verdict first fixed contract liability and proximate causation. It then itemized gross damages as $1,350,000 for cost embodied in the rejected lot, $850,000 for replacement and associated incremental work, and $2,000,000 for bounded delay. The three categories total $4,200,000 without incorporating mitigation.

On Granite Heron’s affirmative defense, jurors found a $300,000 net reduction. The instructions required them to consider reasonable alternative expense before stating a net number and prohibited using the alternative to reduce the original-lot value merely because an earlier campaign might shorten delay. The October 15 judgment therefore was $3,900,000.

Granite Heron’s November 10 renewed motion sought a different deduction. Park treated $1,800,000 of delay as avoidable and subtracted the complete $300,000 transfer estimate, producing $1,500,000. The district court adopted that model, amended the award to $2,700,000, and conditionally ordered another financial trial if JMOL later ceased to control.

The arithmetic is not disputed. The disagreement concerns whether the record compelled each factual input. A mathematically correct model remains only as strong as its assumptions about successful qualification, schedule completion, residual delay, and reasonableness. Those matters were contested by admissible evidence and assigned to the jury.

## Review of renewed judgment is de novo

An appellate court reviews a Rule 50 judgment without deference to the district court’s preferred factfinding. The question is whether, viewing evidence and reasonable inferences in favor of the verdict winner, a reasonable jury had a legally sufficient basis for its answer. Credibility choices and comparative evidentiary weight may not be reassigned to the judge.

That standard differs from Rule 59. A district court considering a new trial may weigh evidence and assess whether a verdict conflicts with the clear weight, rests on false proof, or would produce injustice. The existence of that separate authority confirms that judgment as a matter of law cannot be justified merely because the judge believes another number is better supported.

Granite Heron bore the mitigation burden under North Carolina law. *Thermal Design, Inc. v. M&M Builders, Inc.* places on the breaching party both unreasonable failure to reduce loss and the amount of reduction. *Clark v. Bichsel* likewise treats mitigation as an affirmative defense rather than a shift in Blue Ember’s gross-damages burden.

Accordingly, every reasonable inference about incomplete qualification, technical risk, probability, and timing belongs to Blue Ember at the JMOL stage. The court may not presume a successful December course and require Blue Ember to disprove it. Granite Heron must show that no rational jury could choose a smaller supported net deduction.

## Express preservation does not satisfy the merits burden

The assumed motions give Granite Heron procedural access to Rule 50(b); they do not convert disputed evidence into established fact. Rule 50(a)(2) concerns specification of judgment and grounds. It does not lower the standard for entering that judgment or relieve the movant of an affirmative-defense burden supplied by substantive law.

Granite Heron’s theory depends on several linked propositions: Blue Ember unreasonably declined the slot; transfer would pass the November readiness gate; the campaign would begin December 2; later testing and disposition would proceed sufficiently early; $1,800,000 of bounded delay would be avoided; and the alternative would cost only $300,000. A reasonable doubt about any link changes the net result.

Calder supplied no numerical success probability. The preliminary estimate was not a guaranteed cap, and staged exposure could remain after cancellation. The schedule overlapped tasks to gain time and expressly recognized critical findings, method difficulty, material shortages, or engineering failure. Blue Ember’s witnesses explained why those uncertainties mattered to a validation sponsor.

The jury could credit concrete capacity without treating the most favorable completion path as inevitable. Preservation cannot supply the missing compulsion. It merely prevents Blue Ember from winning on a procedural threshold and requires the Court to apply ordinary de novo review to the evidence actually tried.

## The $300,000 verdict had a rational evidentiary basis

Jurors were not required to reveal their intermediate gross-avoidance figure. They could find a smaller probability-adjusted timing benefit, subtract necessary alternative costs, and reach $300,000 net. They also could discount Park’s assumption that only $200,000 of the bounded delay would remain after a campaign whose final disposition extended beyond manufacturing.

Brooks’s model allowed timing and risk inputs to vary separately. He agreed the option was genuine but explained that the contemporaneous materials lacked a defensible basis for assigning the full claimed success and acceleration. Calder described both capability and open work. Solis described a documented choice among an uncertain earlier path and a qualified later one.

The verdict sits between the parties’ endpoints rather than outside the evidence. It did not find zero mitigation, and it did not accept the complete $1,500,000 defense calculation. Such an intermediate response is permissible where experts share a base model but dispute probability, attribution, and the consequence of unresolved prerequisites.

The district court’s conclusion that no reasonable jury could go below $1,500,000 necessarily treated Park’s successful-path inputs as conclusive. That is evidentiary weighing. The same court’s conditional Rule 59 reasoning may reflect a permissible clear-weight judgment, but it cannot substitute for the more protective Rule 50 inquiry.

## The alternative cost and timing were not fixed ceilings

Cobalt Finch’s $300,000 figure was a working estimate for transfer management, focused qualification, document adaptation, selected analytical work, training, engineering confirmation, and accelerated capacity. The response expressly stated that a major unknown gap could increase scope. Booking also exposed Blue Ember to staged charges if readiness work stopped the project.

Park used the entire estimate as the cost of a successful course, which was arithmetically conservative within her chosen scenario. But it was not evidence that $300,000 capped all risk-adjusted cost across possible outcomes. A jury could account for failure exposure, duplicated staffing, or the continued need for Aurora Quay when evaluating net benefit.

Nor did December 2 equal completion. The proposal projected campaign activity near December 20 before later testing, review, and quality disposition. Comparing manufacturing start dates alone overstates acceleration unless the model consistently maps downstream milestones. Brooks criticized certainty in that mapping without denying that some time could be saved.

These variables directly affect the amount Granite Heron had to prove. If likely gross avoidance was less than $1,800,000, or reasonable alternative expense exceeded the working estimate in probability-weighted terms, the net deduction falls below $1,500,000. The trial evidence permitted both conclusions.

## Rule 50(c) keeps the conditional ruling analytically separate

When a court grants renewed judgment and a new-trial motion is pending, Rule 50(c)(1) requires a conditional ruling. The district court complied by stating that, if JMOL were later vacated or reversed, gross damages and mitigation should be retried together. That determination has a future operative consequence but does not conduct a second trial while JMOL remains effective.

If this panel reverses renewed judgment, Rule 50(c)(2) directs attention to the conditional order and permits appellate instructions. Blue Ember preserves its position that the $300,000 verdict was not against the clear weight and that no retrial was warranted. Granite Heron argues that the monetary subjects are intertwined and that the district court acted within its discretion.

If the panel instead affirms JMOL, the condition never activates. There is then no need to decide whether the district court abused its Rule 59 discretion or properly limited the proposed retrial. An appellate ruling on those dormant questions would add components unrelated to the judgment necessary for affirmance.

The branch’s final disposition therefore must follow the JMOL result. Blue Ember requests reversal and the consequent component analysis. It agrees, however, that an affirmance path should decide only mitigation JMOL and the amended judgment, leaving the conditional order legally present but unreviewed.

## Remedy must reflect the ruling actually made

Reversal of JMOL would remove the $1,500,000 judicial deduction from the amended judgment. The Court would then determine the effect of the conditional new-trial ruling under Rule 50(c), rather than automatically restoring either existing monetary instrument as an immediately payable final recovery. Any mandate should identify the precise operative consequence.

Affirmance would produce a simpler result. The $2,700,000 amended judgment would remain in force, no damages retrial would commence, and the conditional order would stay dormant. The Court should not describe that result as affirming the Rule 59 ruling or its scope because neither issue would have been necessary to decide.

Under either path, contract liability and shipment causation remain untouched. Granite Heron does not seek relief from those separated findings in this counterfactual record, and Blue Ember does not use mitigation to reopen them. The rejected lot, replacement costs, gross delay base, and verdict structure retain their established relationships.

The Court also should reiterate branch isolation. A counterfactual judgment cannot amend the actual appeal, and the preservation premise cannot migrate into the district documents. Any disposition operates only within the never-filed docket and must use the docket number bearing the explicit nonoccurrence designation.

## Conclusion and service certification

Even with express pre-verdict preservation assumed, the evidence did not compel a $1,500,000 deduction. Cobalt Finch offered a genuine and potentially valuable course, but program qualification, downstream timing, cost exposure, and probability remained disputed. The jury’s $300,000 net finding recognized avoidable loss while declining to assume the best possible alternative outcome.

Blue Ember requests reversal of the mitigation JMOL and vacatur of the $2,700,000 amended judgment, followed by the component-specific treatment Rule 50(c) requires. It does not request any change to liability or causation. If the Court affirms JMOL, it should expressly refrain from deciding the dormant conditional new-trial ruling and its possible scope.
