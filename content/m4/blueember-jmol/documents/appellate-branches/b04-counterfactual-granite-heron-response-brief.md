## The Durham offer was operationally real

Cobalt Finch’s October 2 proposal reserved an identified Durham suite until October 4 at 17:00 Eastern Daylight Time. The site had development-biologics experience, appropriate scale, frozen receiving, compatible single-use systems, trained personnel, and access to the analytical support needed for an accelerated BE-214 campaign.

The proposal did not merely invite negotiations. It set an October 7 kickoff, an October 14–15 focused assessment, transfer work through early November, an engineering exercise during November 11–15, a November 22 readiness gate, and a December 2 manufacturing start. Campaign completion was projected near December 20.

Dr. Calder confirmed that the sequence was feasible. No witness identified a fatal equipment conflict, a missing regulatory permission, an unavailable critical material, or a scientific barrier that would prevent transfer. Another sponsor ultimately booked the released slot, confirming that the capacity was not hypothetical inventory.

Blue Ember emphasizes that success was not guaranteed. Mitigation law does not demand a guarantee before a reasonable alternative becomes obligatory. The evidence must be assessed at the time of choice, and the concrete availability, technical fit, schedule, and potential savings made further pursuit necessary rather than optional.

## Qualification work did not make the alternative illusory

Cobalt Finch had not previously manufactured BE-214, so a program-specific comparison, method allocation, document adaptation, training, and engineering confirmation remained. Those tasks were part of the offered accelerated plan. Their existence does not establish that the plan was unsuitable; it explains how a capable facility would become ready.

The qualification file characterized execution risk as elevated but manageable. It identified decision gates and staged expenditure so that Blue Ember could stop if a material incompatibility emerged. That structure reduced exposure while preserving the only known route to a substantially earlier campaign.

Blue Ember released the hold before the focused assessment began. It therefore never learned whether the open items could be resolved on the proposed schedule. A party cannot convert uncertainty created by declining available diligence into proof that no reasonable mitigation step existed.

The jury could recognize some opportunity, but its $300,000 net answer did not rationally account for the uncontested scale of acceleration. Once the proposal’s feasibility, schedule, and limited incremental cost are placed alongside the $2,000,000 bounded delay component, the minimum supported reduction is Park’s $1,500,000 figure.

## The schedule supported substantial avoidance

Aurora Quay’s replacement campaign began March 3, 2025. Cobalt Finch proposed to begin manufacturing on December 2, 2024, roughly three months earlier, with initial campaign work finishing near December 20. Testing, review, and disposition would follow, but those downstream steps also would commence months ahead of the later path.

Park mapped the difference against the bounded delay model. She did not assume that every day between start dates converted automatically into damages. Her analysis left $200,000 of the $2,000,000 delay component unaffected and attributed $1,800,000 to time a successful accelerated course would avoid.

The contemporaneous schedule supported that allocation. Tasks overlapped deliberately, critical inputs had identified owners, and the readiness gate preceded the reserved start. No competing calculation tied the jury’s much smaller net answer to a particular missed milestone, probability, or residual-delay amount.

Brooks described uncertainty but did not supply a supported alternative number. Rule 50 permits a jury to resolve disputed inferences; it does not permit an award assembled without a rational bridge from evidence to amount. The undisputed calendar and bounded model establish a floor that the $300,000 finding failed to respect.

## The economic calculation was bounded and transparent

Granite Heron did not challenge the jury’s $4,200,000 gross total for purposes of its renewed motion. That figure consists of $1,350,000 associated with the rejected validation lot, $850,000 in replacement and related incremental work, and $2,000,000 in bounded delay. Mitigation applies after those components are combined.

Park attributed $1,800,000 of delay to time avoidable through Cobalt Finch. She then deducted the entire $300,000 projected alternative expense rather than ignoring the cost of reasonable mitigation. The resulting $1,500,000 is a net defense amount, not a second recovery or an attack on original-lot value.

Subtracting $1,500,000 from $4,200,000 yields $2,700,000. By contrast, the jury subtracted only $300,000 and produced the October 15 judgment of $3,900,000. Every figure is traceable, and the amended judgment changes no nonmonetary finding.

Blue Ember speculates about additional risk-adjusted expense, but the record supplied no quantified amount beyond the $300,000 working estimate. A verdict cannot manufacture more alternative cost without evidence. Park’s use of the complete disclosed estimate was favorable to Blue Ember and established the minimum net reduction supported by the record.

## The jury’s intermediate number lacked a record bridge

An intermediate verdict is not automatically reasonable merely because it falls between competing endpoints. The record must permit a rational calculation or inference connecting the evidence to that number. Here, no witness explained how a $300,000 net deduction followed from the capacity, schedule, cost, and bounded delay proof.

Jurors could assess uncertainty, but they could not disregard uncontested facts. Cobalt Finch had the suite, the proposed systems were relevant, no fatal conflict appeared, the transfer calendar was concrete, and the later qualified campaign began months afterward. Those facts established much more than a marginal opportunity.

Brooks offered criticism rather than a competing model. He assigned no success probability, alternative completion date, increased transfer expense, or different residual loss. Solis described the decision as prudent from the sponsor’s perspective, yet subjective caution does not quantify the loss a reasonable response would have prevented.

The district court did not choose Park over an equally supported number. It concluded that the evidence, viewed favorably to Blue Ember, could not sustain a reduction below $1,500,000. That conclusion respects the jury’s role while enforcing the requirement that monetary findings rest on a legally sufficient evidentiary foundation.

## Rule 50 review preserves burdens without reweighing

Renewed judgment is reviewed de novo, with reasonable inferences drawn for Blue Ember. The court may not make credibility choices or prefer one supported factual account merely because it appears stronger. Granite Heron accepts that demanding standard and relies on matters that were uncontradicted or insufficiently answered.

North Carolina treats mitigation as an affirmative defense. Under *Thermal Design, Inc. v. M&M Builders, Inc.* and *Clark v. Bichsel*, Granite Heron bears unreasonable failure and the amount avoidable. The district court held that the concrete offer and financial evidence met that burden as a matter of law at the $1,500,000 level.

The holding does not shift a burden to Blue Ember simply because it could not prove certain failure. Blue Ember presented all of its evidence. The defect is that its evidence established open tasks and ordinary execution risk without furnishing a reasonable basis to shrink the demonstrated net benefit to one fifth of Park’s calculation.

Nor did the court resolve Calder’s credibility against Blue Ember. Calder acknowledged incomplete qualification while confirming relevant capability, feasibility, and absence of a known disabling mismatch. Giving Blue Ember every favorable inference from those concessions still leaves the accelerated route and minimum economic consequence intact.

## Blue Ember’s uncertainty arguments do not defeat the floor

Blue Ember emphasizes that initial manufacturing did not equal final disposition. Park’s model accounted for downstream work by leaving part of the bounded delay in place. The $1,800,000 avoidance figure was not the maximum imaginable benefit; it was a measured allocation within a $2,000,000 delay category.

Blue Ember also points to staged cancellation exposure. The $300,000 estimate covered the identified accelerated effort, and Park deducted it in full. No exhibit established a larger expected cost. A factfinder could not increase the expense component solely because the proposal allowed revision if a major unknown appeared.

Finally, Blue Ember invokes hindsight. The district court relied on the October materials, not later success. At the decision point, the suite was held, the schedule was specific, relevant systems existed, and no fatal incompatibility was known. The later booking merely corroborated that scarce capacity was genuine.

These answers do not require certainty. They show that the remaining uncertainties were too general to support reducing an evidenced $1,500,000 net benefit to $300,000. The law tolerates reasonable estimates; it does not allow an unexplained figure to survive solely because scientific transfer always carries some risk.
