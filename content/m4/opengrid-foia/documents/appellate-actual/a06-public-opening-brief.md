## Jurisdiction and Standards of Review

The district court entered final judgment in synthetic docket SYN-DMD-25-CV-0914 on January 2, 2026. Open Grid filed its notice of appeal on February 2, and the record was transmitted on February 9. This Court has jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1291 because the judgment disposed of the parties’ competing claims and left no merits issue pending in the district court.

FOIA exemption rulings resolved on summary judgment receive de novo review. The agency bears the burden of showing that withheld information falls within a claimed exemption, and the reviewing court must assess whether its declarations and indexes provide a reasonably specific basis for that conclusion. A reviewing court may examine controlled material without turning that material into public operational instruction.

Segregability is likewise an independent legal obligation. The record must support a finding that all reasonably segregable nonexempt matter was released after a reasoned, document-sensitive review. Judicial sealing presents a separate access question. Under Company Doe v. Public Citizen, a court must identify the source of the public-access right, consider alternatives, and support restrictions with specific findings. Open Grid challenges the district court’s breadth, not this Court’s carefully bounded interim treatment of four paired filings.

## The Foreseeable-Harm Requirement

FOIA’s foreseeable-harm provision, 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A), requires the agency to reasonably foresee that disclosure would harm an interest protected by an exemption, unless disclosure is prohibited by law. A recital of the exemption’s purpose does not alone establish the consequence of releasing each disputed category.

For the supported decision-tree relationships, the declarations connect disclosure to exposure of how a concealed condition governs an ordered response. That account is sufficiently concrete even in sanitized form. For administrative captions and generic coordination language, however, DOE often says only that context is sensitive. It does not identify a comparable chain between the visible text and a protected harm.

This distinction matters for both review and remedy. Open Grid accepts that the Court may affirm where a functional explanation and predicted harm converge. It asks the Court to vacate rulings that relied on categorical labels without a record-specific bridge. The result would not compel immediate release. On remand, DOE could provide a lawful explanation, release the material, or determine that a narrower redaction adequately protects the asserted interest. Foreseeable harm supplies discipline without forcing operational content into the public record.

## A Category Must Remain Tied to Function

Functional grouping can make a large record understandable. The defect here is not the use of categories as such, but the slippage between a category name and a conclusion. A notice reading `[REDACTED—FICTIONAL DUTY ROLE]` might denote a nonpublic assignment in one passage and an ordinary administrative owner in another. `[REDACTED—FICTIONAL SAFETY REFERENCE]` may point to a genuinely sensitive dependency or merely to a general policy heading.

The public Vaughn account often assigns identical consequences to those distinct settings. It does not consistently state what relationship would be learned, why that relationship is not already apparent from released text, or which exemption supplies the protection. The agency may rely on a representative explanation only when the representation actually fits the grouped passages.

Open Grid seeks a remand instruction that preserves useful categories but requires DOE to test their membership. An entry can state a document family, page function, field type, relevant statutory clause, and predicted consequence without disclosing the hidden content. If that account supports 7(E), withholding remains available. If it supports only a generalized concern, the field must receive a new determination rather than inherit protection from its neighbors.

## Exemption 7(F) Requires More Than a Safety Label

Exemption 7(F) applies to law-enforcement information whose production could reasonably be expected to endanger the life or physical safety of an individual. Open Grid does not dispute that the statute can protect information linked to a real and reasoned safety concern. It contests DOE’s categorical equation of every safety-associated label with the required danger.

The indexes identify fictional duty roles, safety references, and contact channels, but their explanations frequently stop at the possibility that the collection concerns protective work. A public reader cannot tell whether a particular field identifies an exposed person, changes the likelihood of harm, or merely describes an administrative function. The thirty-nine fully withheld pages citing 7(F) received substantially the same account despite appearing in different record families.

EPIC v. Department of Homeland Security permits protection without naming the threatened person, while the statute still requires an expected endangerment of an individual. DOE can make that showing without publishing a live channel. It did not do so categorically here. The district court therefore erred by treating the presence of a safety-themed field as enough to sustain every 7(F) withholding.

## Segregability Remains Independent

FOIA directs agencies to provide any reasonably segregable nonexempt portion after deletion of exempt matter. That sentence is not satisfied merely because some information on a page is properly withheld. The agency must evaluate whether intelligible titles, approval language, revision entries, headings, or explanatory prose can stand without revealing a protected relationship.

DOE’s supplemental release is concrete evidence that smaller units existed. RLS-OG-002 added seventeen passages across eleven replacement pages: six pages gained two passages apiece and five gained one, with no change to the responsive universe. Those releases did not disclose a concealed condition, ordered response, escalation value, person, or channel. They supplied ordinary context that the earlier review had treated as inseparable.

The district court relied too heavily on declarations asserting line-by-line review without reconciling that assertion with the correction or the family-level Vaughn descriptions. Open Grid does not ask the Court to conduct redaction itself. It asks for vacatur and a remand requiring a fresh, documented segregability analysis. Any detail properly protected under 7(E), and any 7(F) detail supported on remand, may remain withheld while meaningful nonexempt text is released.

## Judicial Sealing Is a Separate Inquiry

The district court restricted broad collections of briefing and supporting material largely because they discussed withheld agency information. That approach merged FOIA exemption analysis with the public’s qualified right of access to judicial records. Company Doe requires document-specific consideration, public notice, evaluation of less drastic alternatives, and findings adequate for review.

This Court’s interim paired practice supplies the narrower model. Public briefs and appendix excerpts remain accessible. Controlled counterparts contain only harmless semantic placements, not real protected data, and are identified individually. The motion and certificate explain the reason, scope, alternatives, eligible viewers, and review point. No party seeks a sealed docket or disappearance of public legal reasoning.

Open Grid maintains that even these temporary restrictions must end or narrow when their justification ceases. It challenges the district court’s broader order because it did not distinguish among documents or explain why redaction could not protect the asserted interests. Vacatur would not automatically publish a protected 7(E) relationship. It would require the district court to apply the judicial-record standard independently and make particular findings before imposing any renewed restriction.

## The Proper Remand Is Bounded

The requested remedy preserves what the present record supports. The Court should affirm the withholding of nonpublic trigger relationships, response ordering, and implementation details within the operational decision trees under 7(E). That ruling can be stated at the level of function, without revealing a concealed value or actionable arrangement.

The Court should vacate the categorical 7(F) ruling and remand for a showing tied to the expected endangerment of an individual. It should separately vacate the segregability ruling and direct DOE to reconsider all remaining records in light of the passage-level supplemental release. Finally, it should vacate the district court’s overbroad sealing order and require material-specific findings under the applicable access framework.

None of those remands dictates disclosure of information sustained under 7(E). Nor do they foreclose DOE from supporting a narrower 7(F) withholding or a particular judicial restriction. They restore the sequence required by statute and access doctrine: identify the information, connect it to the governing interest, release what can reasonably stand alone, and restrict a judicial filing only on findings directed to that document.
