## Standards of Review and Proof

We review the legal application of FOIA exemptions de novo. The agency bears the burden of proving that withheld information fits an exemption. Summary judgment may rest on declarations and functional indexes when they are reasonably specific, grounded in personal knowledge, internally consistent, and not contradicted by evidence of bad faith.

Under *Rein v. United States Patent and Trademark Office*, descriptions must permit adversarial testing and meaningful judicial review. Segregability is an affirmative obligation: the agency must disclose any reasonably segregable nonexempt portion after deleting exempt material and show that it performed that task.

Functional grouping is permissible when founded on document-by-document examination and linked to the protected interest. *Zaid v. Department of Justice* does not allow a broad label to replace record analysis. A safety category must still satisfy the text of 7(F).

We review public-access questions independently and evaluate sealing findings under the governing access standard. *Company Doe v. Public Citizen* requires notice, alternatives analysis, and reasons specific enough to support closure. An agency’s FOIA position does not itself establish that a judicial record may be sealed.

## Exemption 7(E) Doctrine

Section 552(b)(7)(E) addresses techniques and procedures and also guidelines whose disclosure could reasonably be expected to risk circumvention of law. In *Grey v. Alfonso-Royals*, we explained that the risk clause modifies guidelines, not techniques and procedures. Nonpublic implementation detail may remain protected even where the general technique is known.

The distinction requires attention to function. A title or administrative sentence is not a technique simply because it appears in an operational document. The agency must identify the implementation relation or qualifying guideline and give a safe explanation of why the withheld content belongs to that category.

Here, public notices mark fictional decision predicates, relative response ordering, and guideline values. The controlled copies replace them with inert terms that communicate type and placement but no content. For guideline fields, DOE separately records the predicted circumvention risk.

The agency also recognizes limits. Approval dates, owners, neutral purpose statements, revision history, and general recordkeeping are disclosed when they remain meaningful. Relational position may be protected when it itself reveals implementation. That content-sensitive approach accords with the statutory text and *Grey*.

## Exemption 7(F) Doctrine

Section 552(b)(7)(F) protects law-enforcement information when disclosure could reasonably be expected to endanger an individual’s life or physical safety. The agency need not identify the endangered person by name. It must still link the withheld information to a reasonable expectation of danger to someone.

*Electronic Privacy Information Center v. Department of Homeland Security* applies that standard and confirms that a valid safety rationale does not eliminate segregability. Categorical treatment is available when meaningful functional groups emerge from document-by-document review and share the relevant harm.

The corrected premise uses four groups: duty assignment, contact category, safety reference, and escalation placement. A retained segment is placed in a group only if it performs the stated function. A generic heading, general coordination sentence, or bare use of safety vocabulary does not qualify.

Dual markings receive two explanations. A segment may reveal an implementation relation under 7(E) and a protective human function under 7(F), but the existence of one does not prove the other. This disciplined structure is necessary because physical safety is a distinct statutory interest.

## Public-Access Principles

Judicial records are not sealed merely because related agency material was withheld under FOIA. A court first identifies the source and strength of public access, provides notice and an opportunity to object, considers less restrictive alternatives, and makes specific findings explaining the closure selected.

*Company Doe* rejects broad secrecy based on generalized sensitivity. When First Amendment access applies, closure must serve a compelling interest and be narrowly tailored. A public redacted counterpart is an important alternative but does not by itself supply the required interest or findings.

The corrected premise respects that sequence. The hypothetical court identifies the access interests and the authentic implementation or human-function information discussed in each judicial passage. It assesses every controlled brief instead of sealing a filing category wholesale.

We review the legal sufficiency of those findings independently. Access technology matters only after the justification is established. Session grants, stable references, verification, and revocation cannot rescue an unsupported order, but they can help enforce a properly bounded one.
