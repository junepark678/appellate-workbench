# Deposition of Blue Cedar Compliance, Inc. — designation and scope

Q. Please identify the witness and the capacity in which she appears today.

A. Mira Solis is appearing on October 9, 2025 as Blue Cedar Compliance, Inc.'s designated
representative under Rule 30(b)(6). This examination is distinct from her September 29 individual
deposition. Blue Cedar prepared her through review of company records and interviews with Draven
Pike, Naomi Voss, and Sable Finch.

Q. What subjects did the company designate her to address?

A. The company's records and preservation systems; the RIF population and savings models; the
written criteria and compliance controls; receipt and routing of Benton's opposition and agency
notice; calibration changes and exceptions; selection authority; post-RIF redistribution;
explanations for the selection; and the completeness of production on those topics.

Q. Are the answers limited to Solis's personal memory?

A. No. Where she says “the company,” she states Blue Cedar's position after reasonable corporate
preparation. Some information came from records or other employees rather than her personal
observation. Counsel and the witness will identify a genuinely unknown matter rather than treating
the distinction between personal and organizational knowledge as a reason not to answer.

# Covered population and headcount arithmetic

Q. State the size of the covered population when the workbook opened.

A. There were twenty-three authorized operations and support positions: twenty-one filled and two
vacant. The manager comparison included Benton, Wynn, Ibarra, and Keene because each managed
regulated-client audits, assigned field personnel, and reviewed remediation work.

Q. What did the final B-7 scenario remove?

A. It removed five filled positions and eliminated the two vacancies. The filled positions were
Benton, an audit scheduling specialist, a controls documentation lead, a client onboarding
analyst, and a vendor coordination specialist. The vacancies were field auditor and project
coordinator.

Q. How many filled positions remained?

A. Sixteen. The reconciliation is twenty-three authorized positions equal twenty-one filled plus
two vacant; sixteen filled equal twenty-one minus five terminations; and sixteen final covered
positions equal twenty-three minus the five terminations and two vacancies. When Blue Cedar used
the phrase “seven positions eliminated,” it did not mean seven people were discharged. Five
occupants were terminated and two unoccupied positions were removed.

# Savings target and modeled alternatives

Q. What savings target did finance establish?

A. $612,000 in annualized payroll and employer-paid benefit savings. B-7 projected $618,400. Its
seven amounts were $148,000, $94,600, $88,800, $82,400, $76,200, $66,400, and $62,000.

Q. Was Benton mathematically required to reach the target?

A. No. The nonmanager position base was $470,400. Replacing Benton with Wynn produced $609,400 in
position savings, Ibarra $602,400, and Keene $598,400. A separately modeled $14,600 archive-hosting
expense consolidation raised those alternatives to $624,000, $617,000, and $613,000.

Q. Why did Blue Cedar select B-7?

A. The company's position is that Pike exercised operational judgment about continuity, summer
travel, automation support, salary, and transferability. Finance confirmed the arithmetic but did
not direct which manager to choose. The archive-hosting amount was an expense saving rather than
a position, and Pike rejected the alternative combinations on coverage grounds. Blue Cedar
therefore concedes that arithmetic alone did not compel Benton's selection.

# RIF criteria and anti-retaliation controls

Q. What criteria governed manager evaluation?

A. Audit completion carried thirty points, client continuity twenty-five, credentials fifteen,
cross-region availability fifteen, and role uniqueness fifteen. Higher totals favored retention.
The protocol required a cited basis and a version entry for manual changes.

Q. What did the anti-retaliation policy require?

A. It prohibited protected activity from influencing timing, criteria, scoring, selection, or
explanation. During the ninety days after notice, human resources was to review proposed changes
affecting the employee and identify the decisionmaker, criteria, contemporaneous support, and
comparators. The policy did not prohibit a genuine restructuring.

Q. Was Benton's protected activity assigned a score?

A. No. The workbook contained no protected-activity field. Her employee-relations status appeared
as a separate human-resources review flag. The absence of a scoring field is not the same as a
finding about motive; it establishes only how the written workbook was structured. Blue Cedar's
corporate position is that Pike did not use protected activity, while Benton disputes that position
through timing, knowledge, calibration, and explanation evidence.

# Corporate notice and decisionmaker knowledge

Q. What does Blue Cedar admit about the January complaint?

A. Benton sent the January 17 message, Voss understood it as an allegation of sex discrimination,
and Solis opened ER-25-014. The company does not contend that the message was merely an ordinary
workload request for purposes of this testimony.

Q. What does the company admit about the agency notice?

A. Blue Cedar received it February 10. Solis routed it to Pike that afternoon. The system recorded
an open event February 11, Pike replied that he had received it, and he attended the February 12
meeting concerning preservation, assignment-score sources, staffing planning, and response
ownership.

Q. Does Blue Cedar maintain that Pike knew nothing about protected activity?

A. No. Consistent with Pike's deposition, the company admits he knew before March 24 that Benton
had raised an internal assignment complaint and that an agency matter existed. Blue Cedar disputes
that he studied every allegation when the packet was opened and denies retaliatory use. The
company's position is therefore a denial of causal use, not a denial that Pike had general
awareness of both matters.

# Calibration changes and comparator exceptions

Q. Summarize the changes to Benton's score.

A. Her imported total of 84 fell to 68. Continuity went from 22 to 14, availability from 12 to 6,
and role uniqueness from 9 to 7. Completion remained 27 and credentials 14. The changed fields
were calibration judgments rather than corrections produced by refreshed quantitative sources.

Q. What happened to the retained comparators?

A. Wynn's continuity increased from 20 to 22 for Lantern, ending May 30. Ibarra's availability
increased from 9 to 14 based on offered future travel despite sixteen completed days. Keene's
uniqueness increased from 14 to 15 for the automation library without a formal transfer-time
estimate. Their final totals were 78, 78, and 75.

Q. What consistency issues did the company identify?

A. Solis flagged the later dates of Benton's transitions, completed versus promised travel, and
the absence of an automation transfer estimate. She certified formula reconciliation, not a
finding that treatment was consistent. Pike approved the entries under “business continuity.”
Blue Cedar authenticates that sequence while maintaining that the judgments were legitimate.

# Selection authority and effective dates

Q. Who approved the manager selected in B-7?

A. Pike approved it on March 21 after Finch confirmed the arithmetic and Solis supplied the
variance audit. Human resources could flag issues, but it did not have authority to substitute a
different manager. Finance could reject a calculation, but it did not command Benton’s selection.

Q. When did Benton's employment end?

A. March 24. The termination letter was effective that day. The April 1 date in the final workbook
was the payroll-model date used for annualized projections; it was not a second termination date.

Q. Was the selection disciplinary?

A. No. The termination register says performance action “none” and rehire eligibility “yes.” The
letter cited budget savings and redistribution of management responsibilities. Blue Cedar's later
position included completion and availability as supporting aspects of the calibrated decision,
but the company identifies no disciplinary record and no contemporaneous client complaint. It
also concedes that the five manager criteria did not operate as a mechanical lowest-score rule.

# Redistribution and explanation history

Q. What happened to Benton's work after March 24?

A. Northline moved to Wynn, Oriole to Ibarra, the remediation-review queue to Keene, and the
monthly quality meeting to Voss. The March 25 message said clients should be told the change was
organizational alignment rather than a quality concern. No new audit-manager position was created.

Q. Did the underlying functions end?

A. No. The clients and review work remained active. By May 31, the ledger showed transferred
matters continuing and forty percent of Benton's recorded client hours assigned to Wynn,
thirty-five percent to Ibarra, fifteen percent to Keene, and ten percent to Voss.

Q. How did the stated rationale develop?

A. The March 20 email emphasized salary and management consolidation. The March 24 letter used
budget savings and redundancy. The April response added relative completion and availability.
Pike's July interview added client confidence, though no client complaint was identified. Blue
Cedar characterizes these as details of one RIF reason; it concedes the additional terms appeared
at different times and that their dated records must remain distinct.
