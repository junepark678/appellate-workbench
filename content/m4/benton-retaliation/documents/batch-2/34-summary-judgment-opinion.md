> **FICTIONAL TRAINING ARTIFACT — NOT FILED — NOT LEGAL ADVICE**

UNITED STATES DISTRICT COURT FOR THE EASTERN DISTRICT OF VIRGINIA
Richmond Division — Civil Action No. SYN-EDVA-25-CV-0412

# MEMORANDUM OPINION

Entered December 19, 2025.

Leora Benton claims that Blue Cedar Compliance, Inc. selected her for a March 2025 reduction in
force because she opposed a sex-based assignment practice and filed an EEOC charge. Blue Cedar
moves for summary judgment. It accepts for this motion that Benton's January 17 writing and
February 3 charge were protected and that her termination was adverse. It maintains that an
actual payroll target, calibrated coverage needs, and elimination of a management position—not
retaliation—caused the selection.

The record presents close questions about how Blue Cedar documented several score changes and
explained its choice over time. But Rule 56 requires evidence from which a reasonable factfinder
could conclude that the employer's stated reason was not its real reason and that retaliation was
a but-for cause. Considering the admitted record in Benton's favor, the Court concludes that the
identified irregularities do not cross that threshold. The excluded Wynn declaration is not part
of the merits record. Blue Cedar's motion will be granted for the reasons below.

# Procedural background and admitted record

Benton filed her complaint on June 16, 2025, asserting one Title VII retaliation count and
demanding a jury. Blue Cedar answered on July 7. The Court adopted the parties' proposed schedule
on August 13, setting an October 10 discovery close, an October 17 supplementation deadline, and
the present briefing dates. The parties produced the employee-relations file, notice-routing
history, RIF workbook and version log, comparator scorecards, calibration minutes, performance
records, explanation communications, and headcount ledger. They deposed Benton, Pike, Solis,
Wynn, Ibarra, Voss, and Blue Cedar's corporate representative.

On December 1, the Court excluded only Wynn's newly asserted March 7 motive-related conversation
under Rules 26 and 37. The ruling preserved her testimony about disclosed comparator subjects.
The parties then submitted a joint chart distinguishing admitted facts, genuine disputes, and the
excluded subject. Neither side requests additional discovery on the summary-judgment motion.
Unless otherwise stated, the facts described here are undisputed or are presented in the manner
most favorable to Benton, with reasonable inferences drawn for her and without treating a pleading
allegation as proof.

# Protected activity, notice, and decision authority

On January 17, Benton wrote to supervisor Naomi Voss and human-resources director Mira Solis.
She described what she considered a sex-based pattern in assignment of high-visibility audit-lead
credit, identified examples, requested a neutral review, and asked the company to pause reliance
on the metric. Voss acknowledged and forwarded the writing. Solis opened employee-relations file
ER-25-014 and reminded Voss that proposed changes affecting Benton required human-resources
review. Benton supplied additional data and participated in a January 29 meeting.

Benton filed an EEOC charge on February 3. Blue Cedar received the notice and charge on February
10. Solis routed the packet to Draven Pike, the operations vice president who made the final
manager-selection decision. The system recorded delivery that afternoon, an open event the next
morning, and Pike's reply that he would preserve operations files. He attended a February 12
meeting titled “EEOC / staffing response” and asked when Benton had made her internal complaint.
Pike testified that he knew before March 24 that Benton had raised an assignment issue and that an
EEOC matter existed, while denying that he used either fact in the RIF.

<!-- PAGE BREAK -->
# The RIF target and competing scenarios

Finance director Sable Finch supplied a target of $612,000 in annualized payroll and benefit
savings. The workbook opened February 14 and covered twenty-three authorized positions: twenty-one
filled positions and two vacancies. A February 24 revision added a vendor-coordination specialist
to the positions under consideration. The final B-7 scenario ended five filled positions,
including Benton's $148,000 position, and eliminated two vacancies. It projected $618,400 in
annualized savings, exceeding the target by $6,400.

The workbook also modeled alternatives that retained Benton and selected Wynn, Ibarra, or Keene.
The position savings in those alternatives fell below $612,000, but each reached the target when
paired with a separately modeled $14,600 archive-hosting expense consolidation. Pike rejected
those combinations because he assessed greater continuity, summer-travel, or automation risk.
He approved B-7 on March 21. Blue Cedar terminated Benton on March 24 and redistributed her work
among the three retained managers and Voss. The parties agree that the savings objective was
real. They dispute whether that objective and the asserted coverage judgments truthfully explain
why Benton was the manager selected.

<!-- PAGE BREAK -->
# Calibration and comparator evidence

The February 14 import gave Benton 84 points, the highest of the four compared managers. Wynn
had 76, Ibarra 73, and Keene 74. During calibration, Benton's continuity score fell from 22 to 14,
availability from 12 to 6, and role uniqueness from 9 to 7, leaving a final score of 68. Voss
entered the continuity and availability changes; Solis entered the uniqueness change; Pike
approved the variances. Benton was not interviewed before those changes.

The retained managers received favorable changes or exceptions. Wynn's continuity increased for
a transition ending in May, although Benton's transitions extended into June. Ibarra's availability
increased based on volunteered future travel even though Benton completed more travel in 2024.
Keene received maximum uniqueness credit for maintaining automation scripts without a formal
transfer estimate. Final scores were Wynn 78, Ibarra 78, and Keene 75. The four managers shared a
supervisor, a common scoring rubric, and overlapping audit duties, but they also held different
portfolio responsibilities. The version log preserved the changes, and Solis flagged inconsistent
measurement windows and unsupported distinctions before Pike approved them as business-continuity
judgments.

<!-- PAGE BREAK -->
# Performance, redistribution, and explanations

Benton's annual reviews were positive. She timely closed forty-seven of fifty audits in 2024,
completed twenty-eight travel days, held relevant credentials, and had no disciplinary record.
The termination register marked her eligible for rehire. After termination, Wynn received
Northline, Ibarra received Oriole, Keene received the remediation-review queue, and Voss chaired
the monthly quality meeting. No new manager replaced Benton, but her underlying work continued
and required additional management time during the transition.

The March 24 letter identified budget reduction and redistribution of redundant management work.
Blue Cedar's April EEOC response also discussed salary, coverage, completion, and availability.
During a July litigation-hold interview, Pike referred to salary, redundancy, completion,
willingness to travel, and client confidence. He identified no contemporaneous client complaint
about Benton and explained that “client confidence” meant continuity risk. Benton characterizes
the later accounts as shifting reasons. Blue Cedar characterizes them as detail underlying the
same budget-and-coverage decision. The contemporaneous March 20 email cited savings, redundant
management work, and the retained managers' continuity, travel, and automation coverage, but did
not label Benton a deficient performer.

# Summary-judgment standard

Rule 56 permits judgment when the movant shows that no genuine dispute of material fact exists
and that it is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. The Court views admissible evidence and
reasonable inferences in the nonmovant's favor. It does not assess witness credibility, choose
among competing supported accounts, or require the nonmovant to prove her case at the motion
stage. A factual disagreement is material only if it could affect the result under governing law,
and it is genuine only if the record would permit a reasonable factfinder to return a verdict for
the nonmovant.

The Court considers the record as a whole. Temporal proximity, comparative treatment, procedural
departures, and changes in explanation may reinforce one another even if no single category is
decisive. At the same time, speculation and disagreement with business judgment do not create a
triable question without evidence connecting the challenged decision to the prohibited reason.
The Court applies those principles to the evidence the parties timely disclosed and properly
placed in the record. The prior exclusion order removes only the new Wynn subject and does not
diminish the timely scorecards, depositions, or business records.

# Title VII retaliation framework

Title VII prohibits an employer from discriminating against an employee because she opposed an
unlawful practice or participated in a Title VII proceeding. Benton proceeds through the
burden-shifting method discussed in *Foster v. University of Maryland-Eastern Shore*, 787 F.3d
243 (4th Cir. 2015). She first must establish protected activity, materially adverse action, and
the required causal connection. The employer then must articulate a legitimate, nonretaliatory
reason. The ultimate burden remains with Benton to show that the stated reason was pretextual and
that retaliation was a but-for cause of the challenged action.

The ultimate inquiry does not demand direct evidence or require Benton to disprove every business
consideration. Evidence that the employer's account is unworthy of belief may support an inference
that the prohibited reason was the real one. But the existence of some error, inconsistency, or
unfairness does not itself establish retaliatory causation. The evidence must permit a reasonable
finding that, absent the protected activity, Blue Cedar would not have selected Benton in this
RIF. That is the issue on which the present motion turns.

<!-- PAGE BREAK -->
# Prima-facie showing and legitimate reason

For this motion, the first-stage showing is satisfied. Benton's January 17 writing expressly
opposed a practice she described as sex based, and her February charge constituted protected
participation. Termination was materially adverse. Pike received the charge packet, replied to
the preservation request, attended the February 12 meeting, asked about the internal complaint,
and admitted awareness of both an assignment concern and an EEOC matter before the decision.
The Court therefore does not adopt Blue Cedar's narrower suggestion that only proof Pike read
every allegation would establish knowledge. The six-week interval between receipt and termination
also supplies temporal support at the initial stage.

Blue Cedar has articulated a legitimate reason: it sought a documented $612,000 savings target
and chose a scenario that exceeded the target while, in Pike's judgment, preserving continuity,
travel capacity, and automation support. The company produced contemporaneous finance figures,
workbook versions, meeting minutes, and a redistribution plan. Benton does not dispute the target
or the elimination of other positions. The burden therefore returns to Benton to show a genuine
dispute over pretext and ultimate but-for causation.

<!-- PAGE BREAK -->
# Calibration and comparator proof at the pretext stage

Benton has identified genuine irregularities. Her score dropped sixteen points without a new
source metric, while retained managers received forward-looking credits that were not applied to
her. The written protocol called for common measurement windows, concrete exception support, and
consistent treatment. Solis's audit flagged variances, and Pike approved them without interviewing
Benton. Those facts reasonably permit criticism of the calibration's rigor.

The Court nevertheless concludes that they do not permit a finding that retaliation was the real
reason. The workbook preserved the imported and changed values rather than concealing them. The
minutes identify continuity, travel, and transferability as the subjects discussed. Wynn, Ibarra,
and Keene performed overlapping manager work, but each also carried a responsibility Pike elected
to preserve. The record contains no admitted statement that Voss, Solis, or Pike altered a score
because Benton complained. Knowledge by participants and imperfect adherence to an internal
protocol, without evidence that the imperfection tracked protected activity, leave the asserted
retaliatory connection inferential. A factfinder could reject Pike's business judgments, but that
possibility alone does not show those judgments were a cover for retaliation.

<!-- PAGE BREAK -->
# Alternatives, timing, and the explanation sequence

The target-reaching alternatives likewise do not establish pretext. Each substituted a different
manager and depended on an expense consolidation outside the payroll-position total. Pike testified
that he rejected them because of different operational risks. The alternatives show arithmetic did
not compel Benton's selection, but a discretionary choice among qualifying plans is not evidence
of retaliation unless the record supports the prohibited reason for that choice.

Timing favors Benton at the prima-facie stage: the workbook opened four days after notice, and
termination followed six weeks after receipt. Yet the calibration record describes a company-wide
exercise involving seven positions and a finance target Benton accepts as genuine. The explanation
sequence also permits competing characterizations. The termination letter's budget and redundancy
language was general; later references to completion, availability, and continuity correspond to
criteria present in the protocol and workbook before termination. “Client confidence” lacked a
supporting complaint, but Pike connected that phrase to continuity rather than misconduct. On this
record, the later detail does not reasonably establish that Blue Cedar abandoned one reason and
invented another.

<!-- PAGE BREAK -->
# Combined record and disposition

The Court has considered the proof cumulatively rather than isolating each fact. Benton engaged in
protected activity; Pike knew of it; the RIF began soon after notice; her high imported score fell;
retained managers received exceptions; other combinations met the target; and later explanations
were more detailed than the termination letter. Those facts create suspicion and would permit a
factfinder to question whether Blue Cedar used its best or most consistent process. They do not,
however, supply a reasonable basis to find that retaliation was the but-for reason for selecting
Benton rather than one of the other managers.

The admitted contemporaneous record consistently identifies a genuine savings objective,
redistribution of Benton's duties, and preservation of functions assigned to the retained managers.
Pike's awareness does not itself prove retaliatory use, and no admitted motive statement bridges
that gap. Because Benton's evidence does not permit the ultimate causal finding required by
*Foster*, Blue Cedar is entitled to judgment as a matter of law on the sole claim. The motion for
summary judgment is granted. A separate final judgment will be entered, and the Clerk will close
the civil action.
